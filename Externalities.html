PART ONE

Externalities and efficiency

Costs and benefits that fall on people outside the trade.

ExternalitiesDefinitions

Private cost, social cost, and social surplus

Externality
An external cost or benefit that falls on bystanders, people other than the buyer or seller. A negative externality is an external cost; a positive externality is an external benefit.

Social cost= private cost + external cost

  • Private cost is the cost paid by the consumer or producer. Social cost is the cost to everyone.
  • Social surplus = consumer surplus + producer surplus + everyone else’s surplus. The efficient quantity maximizes it. Who bears the cost doesn’t change what that quantity is.
  • A market maximizes CS + PS. When there are externalities, that isn’t the same as maximizing social surplus, so the market is inefficient.
ExternalitiesMotivating example

Antibiotics

  • Each use of an antibiotic breeds more resistant bacteria.
  • That is a cost borne by people who didn’t take the antibiotic.
  • The price doesn’t include that external cost, so the price is too low and antibiotics are overused.
Private or external?
The $50 you pay for a jacket is a private cost: you are the one who pays it. The resistant bacteria from your antibiotic are an external cost: other people bear it.
PART TWO

Negative externalities

External costs push the market to produce too much.

Negative externalitiesExternal costs

The market produces more than the efficient quantity

  • With an external cost, the true cost to society, the marginal social cost (MSC), sits above the private supply curve, the marginal private cost (MPC).
  • The gap between them is the external cost per unit.
  • The market produces where demand meets MPC: Qmkt.
  • The efficient quantity is where demand meets MSC: Q*.
  • Since Qmkt > Q*, the good is overproduced. The triangle between MSC and demand, from Q* to Qmkt, is the deadweight loss.

Negative externalitiesThe fix

The Pigouvian tax

Pigouvian tax
A tax on a good with external costs, set equal to the external cost, so the price reflects the true social cost.

Named for Arthur C. Pigou, who first showed how taxes could correct externalities.

  • A tax on an ordinary good increases deadweight loss.
  • A tax on a good with an external cost reduces deadweight loss and raises revenue, because it pushes the market to Q*.
PART THREE

Positive externalities

External benefits leave the market producing too little.

Positive externalitiesExternal benefits

The market produces less than the efficient quantity

External benefit
A benefit received by people other than the buyers and sellers in the market.
  • The true benefit to society, the marginal social benefit (MSB), sits above private demand (MPB).
  • The market stops at Qmkt, but Q* is larger. The good is underproduced.
Vaccines (and COVID-19)
A flu shot protects you and makes you less likely to infect others. That second part is the external benefit, so left to the market, too few people get vaccinated. Economists estimated the external benefit of a COVID-19 vaccine course at $5,800 or more, while the vaccine cost only $20–$40. Governments subsidized production and made the vaccine free to consumers.

PART FOUR

Private solutions: the Coase theorem

When the people involved can bargain their way to the efficient outcome.

Private solutionsThe Coase theorem

Bargaining can fix an externality

Internalizing an externality
Adjusting incentives so decision-makers weigh all the costs and benefits of their actions, private and external.
Coase theorem
If transaction costs are low and property rights are clearly defined, private bargaining will reach the efficient outcome on its own, even with externalities.
  • Transaction costs are everything it takes to reach a deal: finding the parties, bargaining, writing the contract.
  • The catch: for many externalities, such as air pollution over millions of people, transaction costs are high and property rights unclear, so private bargains alone won’t solve every problem.
  • If we can define property rights and lower transaction costs, a new market in the externality can emerge.

PART FIVE

Government solutions

Pigouvian taxes and subsidies, and command and control.

Government solutionsCommand and control

Command and control

Command and control
The government simply orders firms to make or use less.
  • Example: a 2007 rule required washing machines to use 21% less electricity.
The drawback
Regulators often lack the information to pick the cheapest way to hit the goal. So command and control is inflexible and costly compared with a tax, which lets each firm decide how much to cut.

Questions to answer without notes

QUESTION 1

Is the $50 you pay for a jacket a private cost or an external cost?

A private cost. You, the buyer, pay it.

QUESTION 2

What does a Pigouvian tax on a good with external costs do to deadweight loss?

It reduces deadweight loss, by moving the market to Q*.

QUESTION 3

With external benefits, is market output too high or too low?

Too low. The good is underproduced.

QUESTION 4

What does command and control do?

It orders firms to produce (or use) less.

QUESTION 5

Why is command and control often costlier than a tax?

Regulators often lack the information to pick the cheapest way to hit the goal. A tax lets each firm decide how much to cut.

QUESTION 6

When can private bargaining fix an externality?

When transaction costs are low and property rights are clearly defined (the Coase theorem).

Takeaway

Summary

Efficiency

With externalities, the market quantity isn’t the efficient quantity. External costs cause overproduction; external benefits cause underproduction.

Prices

Prices don’t signal true costs and benefits, but Pigouvian taxes and subsidies can fix that. A tax on an external-cost good reduces deadweight loss.

Regulation

Command and control can work but is often costly and inflexible.

Coase

If property rights are clear and transaction costs low, private bargaining can fix externalities without government.
