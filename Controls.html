Price Ceilings and Floors

What happens when the law sets a maximum or minimum price: the five effects of a ceiling, the four effects of a floor, and the arguments for controls.

Examples: rent control, gas lines, the minimum wage, airline regulationDrag the sliders on the live diagrams
Price controlsThe big idea

A legally binding maximum or minimum price

Price ceiling
A maximum price allowed by law. Examples: rent control, gasoline in the 1970s.
Price floor
A minimum price allowed by law. Example: the minimum wage.
  • Controls interfere with market signals. The price can no longer do its job of rationing goods and directing resources.
  • They delink some markets and link others in counterproductive ways, so the damage rarely stays in one market.
  • A control only binds when it is on the “wrong” side of equilibrium: a ceiling below the market price, or a floor above it. Otherwise nothing happens.

PART ONE

Price ceilings and their five effects

Shortages, lower quality, wasteful lines, lost gains from trade, and misallocation of resources.

Price ceilingsOverview

A binding ceiling creates five effects

1. Shortages

Quantity demanded is greater than quantity supplied.

2. Lower quality

Sellers cut quality or service instead of raising the price.

3. Lines and search

Buyers pay with time, bribes and search effort instead of money.

4. Deadweight loss

Trades that would benefit both sides never happen.

5. Misallocation

Goods and resources don’t go to their highest-valued uses.

Effect 2Reductions in product quality

If sellers can’t raise the price, they cut quality

  • At the controlled price there is excess demand.
  • Sellers can’t legally raise the price, so they cut quality or service instead.
  • A cut in quality at the same price is an effective price increase.
The shrinking matzo ball (1972)
During the Nixon-era price controls, AFL-CIO boss George Meany complained that the number of matzo balls in his favorite soup had fallen from four to three. In effect, a price increase that the controls were supposed to prevent.
Effect 3Wasteful lines and search costs

Buyers pay in other ways

Buyers are willing to pay more than the ceiling, but a higher money price is illegal.

  • Bribes: the extra payment goes to the supplier. That is a transfer, not pure waste.
  • Waiting in line: the cost is the value of buyers’ time, and it goes to no one. That is pure waste.
Gas-line logic
If buyers are willing to pay $3 a gallon but the ceiling is lower, the line grows until money price + value of time = $3. Buyers are no better off than paying $3, and the time is simply lost.

Effect 4Lost gains from trade

Deadweight loss

Deadweight loss (DWL)
The reduction in total surplus caused by a market distortion or inefficiency.
  • Below Q*, buyers still value units more than sellers’ cost to produce them.
  • Those are mutually profitable trades that the control blocks.
  • The lost consumer surplus plus the lost producer surplus is the deadweight loss: the triangle between demand and supply, from the quantity traded up to Q*.
Effect 5Misallocation of resources

Resources don’t go to their highest-valued uses

  • With prices frozen, resources don’t flow to where they are valued most.
  • Cold-winter heating oil: a cold snap raises the value of oil in the north, but buyers can’t bid up the price. There is no signal and no incentive to ship oil where it is needed most.
Advanced: random allocation
In a free market the good goes to the highest-valued uses. Under a control it may be handed out randomly, so a high-value buyer is no more likely to get it than a low-value one. Consumer surplus falls toward the average value, well below the free-market result.

Price controlsSpreading across markets

The damage spreads to other markets

  • A shortage in one market causes breakdowns and shortages in others, including markets that have no price control.
  • With many controls, shortages can pop up anywhere at any time.
The energy crisis
Shortages of steel drilling equipment made it hard to expand oil production, during the worst energy crisis in U.S. history.
PART TWO

Case study: rent control

A price ceiling on rental housing.

Rent controlHow the shortage grows

The shortage gets worse over time

Rent control
A price ceiling on rental housing.
  • Controls usually start with a rent freeze. As market rents rise, the frozen rent falls further below equilibrium.
Short run
The supply of apartments is inelastic. Landlords have little choice but to accept the lower rent, so the shortage is small.
Long run
Supply is much more elastic. Fewer units are built, buildings convert to condos, and some are torn down. The shortage grows.

Rent controlThe other effects

The other effects show up too

  • Quality falls: maintenance costs rise and owners cut back. Strong controls turn buildings into slums, and eventually abandoned shells.
  • Search costs and discrimination: with more renters than apartments, landlords can pick and choose. Illegal bribes get disguised as “key money” or a charge for a supposedly “furnished” apartment.
  • Misallocation: apartments don’t go to those who value them most. Some high-value renters can’t get enough housing, while some low-value renters occupy more than they otherwise would.
A softer version: rent regulation
In the 1990s many U.S. cities eased rent control into rent regulation. Rent can rise, but only up to a cap (for example, 10% per year). Because landlords can usually pass along cost increases, the incentive to cut maintenance is much weaker.
Price controlsArguments for controls

Arguments for price controls

ARGUMENT 1

“They help the poor”

Vouchers are a better tool. They don’t create a shortage, and they can be targeted to the poor.

ARGUMENT 2

Disciplining a monopoly

The strongest case. A well-set ceiling can push a monopolist toward the competitive price and quantity.

ARGUMENT 3

Politics

Controls may persist because the public doesn’t connect them with the shortages and quality declines they cause.

PART THREE

Price floors and their four effects

Surpluses, lost gains from trade, wasteful increases in quality, and misallocation of resources.

Price floorsOverview

A binding floor is the mirror image of a ceiling

1. Surpluses

Quantity supplied is greater than quantity demanded.

2. Deadweight loss

Trades that would benefit both sides never happen.

3. Wasteful quality

Sellers compete on quality buyers wouldn’t pay for, since they can’t compete on price.

4. Misallocation

Low-cost producers are kept out, and resources don’t go to their best uses.

Effects 3 & 4Regulated airlines, 1938–1978

Wasteful quality and blocked entry

EFFECT 3: WASTEFUL INCREASES IN QUALITY

Competing on meals, not fares

U.S. airline fares were regulated above the price airlines were willing to sell at. Unregulated fares were about half the regulated price for similar flights. Forbidden to compete on price, airlines competed on quality: lavish meals and service that passengers wouldn’t have paid for otherwise.

EFFECT 4: MISALLOCATION OF RESOURCES

Low-cost carriers kept out

Entry was also restricted, keeping low-cost carriers out of the industry. That blocked the innovations, new ideas and experiments that are part of the competitive market process.

Questions to answer without notes

QUESTION 1

Why does a ceiling below the market price create a shortage?

At the controlled price, quantity demanded is greater than quantity supplied (Qd > Qs).

QUESTION 2

Why are resources misallocated under a price ceiling?

The price can’t rise to signal the shortage, so resources don’t move to their highest-valued uses.

QUESTION 3

Can the effects of a price control reach markets without controls?

Yes. Shortages in one market cause breakdowns and shortages in others.

QUESTION 4

Why do rent-control shortages get worse over time?

Long-run supply is more elastic than short-run supply, so the gap between Qd and Qs grows.

QUESTION 5

What does a binding price floor create?

A surplus. In the labor market, a surplus of labor is unemployment.

QUESTION 6

What is a better way than rent control to help the poor?

Vouchers. They don’t create a shortage and can be targeted to the poor.

Takeaway

Summary

Ceilings

A binding price ceiling creates shortages, lower quality, wasteful lines, deadweight loss, and misallocation.

Spreading

The damage isn’t confined to the controlled market. It can spread across the economy.

Rent control

Shows every effect, and the shortage grows over time as supply becomes more elastic.

Floors

A binding price floor creates surpluses, deadweight loss, wasteful quality increases, and misallocation.

Minimum wage

Above the market wage it creates a surplus of labor, unemployment. Vouchers or targeted aid usually work better than controls.

Key terms

Price ceilingA legal maximum price. Binds when set below the market price.
Price floorA legal minimum price. Binds when set above the market price.
ShortageQd > Qs, the result of a binding ceiling.
SurplusQs > Qd, the result of a binding floor (for example, unemployment).

Rent controlA price ceiling on rental housing. Shortages worsen over time.
Rent regulationA cap on rent increases (for example, 10% a year) that allows cost pass-through.
Minimum wageA price floor on labor. Above the market wage, it creates unemployment.
