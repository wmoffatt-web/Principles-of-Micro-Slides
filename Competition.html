Principles of Microeconomics · Chapter 11

Costs and Profit Maximization Under Competition

Three decisions every producer faces — what price, what quantity, and whether to be in the industry at all — worked out on an oil field where price is the one thing you cannot choose.

Running example: a small oil producer at $50 / barrelDrag the sliders on the live diagrams
Introduction

Every producer answers three questions

QUESTION 1

What price to set?

In a competitive industry the answer is handed to you: the market price. The interesting work is everything that follows from that.

QUESTION 2

What quantity to produce?

Produce until the revenue from one more barrel equals the cost of that barrel — until MR = MC.

QUESTION 3

When to enter or exit?

Compare price to average cost in the long run, and to average variable cost when deciding whether to run the pumps today.

Answering all three for a competitive industry is what builds a supply curve.

PART ONE

What price to set?

A competitive firm is a price taker. Its own demand curve is flat at the market price, and that single fact drives the rest of the chapter.

What price to set?Price takers

You can sell all you want — but only at $50

  • The firm accepts the price the market determines.
  • It can sell all its output at that price.
  • It can sell nothing above that price.
  • It has no reason to go below it — it can already sell everything.
Perfectly elastic demand
The firm’s own demand curve is horizontal at the market price, even though market demand slopes down.

A producer selling 10 barrels out of 82,000,000 is invisible to the market price. Its slice of the demand curve is flat.

What moves you along the spectrum is how much influence a single firm has over price — which you can read straight off the slope of the demand curve it faces. Real industries rarely sit exactly at either end; “competitive” just means firms don’t have much influence.

What price to set?Conditions & time horizon

When is an industry competitive?

  • The product is similar across sellers.
  • There are many buyers and sellers, each small relative to the total market.
  • There are many potential sellers — the ones not yet in the industry discipline price too.
  • Market demand is most elastic in the long run.
Short run
The time period before exit or entry can occur.
Long run
The time after all exit or entry has occurred.

These two horizons are doing real work later: fixed costs are unavoidable in the short run and fully avoidable in the long run.

PART TWO

What quantity to produce?

Profit is total revenue minus total cost. There are two ways to find its maximum — and one of them generalizes to everything else in economics.

Maximizing profitWhich costs count

Ignore what you can’t change

The general principle of rational choice: focus only on what your decision can actually move.

Sunk cost
A cost that cannot be recovered. Sunk costs are never relevant, because no choice you make now can change them.
Fixed cost
A cost that does not vary with the quantity produced. Unchangeable by short-run choices — so ignore it in the short run; changeable in the long run — so focus on it there.
Variable cost
A cost that varies with output. The cost of the next barrel is always a variable cost.
Total cost
Everything it takes to produce a given quantity.TC = FC + VC
Maximizing profitAccounting vs. economic profit

Accountants miss the costs no one invoices

Explicit cost
A cost that requires a money outlay.
Implicit cost
A cost with no outlay of money — an opportunity cost.
  • Maximizing profit means counting both — including the value of the alternatives you gave up.
  • Accountants typically don’t count every opportunity cost, so accounting profit usually exceeds economic profit.
  • Economic profit asks whether this is the firm’s best use of its assets. That is the number a firm wants to maximize.
Accounting profit
TR − explicit costs
Economic profit
TR − (explicit + implicit)
What quantity to produce?Two methods

Two ways to maximize the gap between TR and TC

π = TR − TC profit = total revenue − total cost

METHOD 1 — TOTALS

Find the quantity with the biggest TR − TC

Compute TR and TC at every quantity and read off the widest gap. Correct — but it tells you nothing about why π peaks where it does.

METHOD 2 — MARGINS

Compare the next barrel’s revenue to its cost

Keep going while one more barrel adds more to TR than to TC. The barrel where that stops is the one that maximizes π. This is the method that generalizes.

Total revenue
Price times quantity sold.TR = P × Q
Marginal revenue
The change in TR from selling one more unit. For a competitive firm, MR = P.MR = ΔTR / ΔQ
Marginal cost
The change in TC from producing one more unit.MC = ΔTC / ΔQ

PART THREE

Profits and the average cost curve

A firm can maximize profit and still be losing money. To see profitability on a diagram, you need cost per unit.

Average costDefinitions

Profit = (P − AC) × Q

Average profit per unit, times the number of units. The whole profit diagram is one rectangle.

Average cost (AC or ATC)
Total cost per unit.AC = TC / Q
Average variable cost (AVC)
Variable cost per unit.AVC = TVC / Q
Average fixed cost (AFC)
Fixed cost per unit — always falling, since a fixed number is spread over more units.AFC = TFC / Q
Where MC meets the averages
MC cuts both AVC and AC at their minimums. Above the average it pulls the average up; below it, down.

At the oil firm’s profit-maximizing 8 barrels, AC = $206 / 8 = $25.75, so profit = ($50 − $25.75) × 8 = $194 — the same number the table gave.

PART FOUR

Entry, exit, and industry supply curves

Profit maximization explains one firm. Entry and exit explain the price — and the shape of the industry’s supply curve.

Entry, exit, shutdownThe long run

Zero profit means normal profit

  • Firms enter when P > AC.
  • Firms exit when P < AC.
  • When P = AC, profits are zero and nobody has a reason to move.
“Zero” profit isn’t nothing
Zero economic profit means the price is just enough to pay labor and capital their opportunity costs. Everyone is earning exactly what they could earn elsewhere — a normal profit.
  • Exit usually cannot happen immediately. In the short run the firm owes its fixed costs whether it operates or not.
  • So fixed costs don’t influence short-run decisions at all.
  • The firm should shut the pumps off today only if TR < VC — if the revenue doesn’t even cover the variable cost of producing.
Divide both sides by Q
TR < VC  ⇔  P < AVCShut down when price falls below minimum average variable cost.

Industry supplyIncreasing cost

The cheap oil gets pumped first

Increasing cost industry
Industry costs rise as industry output rises — shown by an upward-sloping supply curve.
  • Greater quantities can be produced only with more expensive methods.
  • Any industry that buys a large fraction of an increasing cost industry’s output inherits increasing costs.

Firm 1’s oil is near the surface; Firm 2 has to drill deep. Firm 2 only enters when the price rises enough to cover its higher minimum average cost — which is why the industry supply curve slopes up.

Industry supplyConstant & decreasing cost

When expansion doesn’t move costs at all

Constant cost industry
Industry costs don’t change with output — a flat long-run supply curve.
  • It meets the conditions for a competitive industry.
  • It demands only a small share of its major inputs, so it can expand or contract without moving input prices.
  • Price is driven down to AC, so profits are driven to normal levels — and price barely moves when the industry grows or shrinks.
Decreasing cost industry
Industry costs fall as output rises — a downward-sloping supply curve. Rare.
  • Clusters do this: as the industry grows, input suppliers move nearby and costs fall.
    • Dalton, Georgia — “Carpet Capital of the World”
    • Silicon Valley — computer technology
  • The cost reductions are temporary. Once the cluster is established, constant or increasing costs are the norm.

Takeaway

What to carry out of Chapter 11

What price to set?

In a competitive industry, the firm sets its price at the market price. It is a price taker.

What quantity to produce?

Produce the quantity that makes P = MC. Since MR = P for a competitive firm, that is the same as MR = MC.

When to shut down?

In the short run, shut down only if price is below average variable cost. Fixed costs are already sunk for today.

When to enter or exit?

In the long run, enter if P > AC and exit if P < AC. Entry and exit drive price toward minimum average cost.

Why supply slopes

Profit maximization plus entry and exit are the supply curve. Costs that rise with industry output give an upward slope; costs that don’t give a flat one; and in rare clustered industries, costs fall and supply slopes down.
