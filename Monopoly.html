Monopoly and Market Power

A firm facing a downward-sloping demand curve has to cut the price to sell one more unit. That single fact puts marginal revenue below price — and everything else in this chapter follows from it.

PART ONE

What market power is

Not the power to charge anything you like — the power to charge above marginal cost without someone undercutting you.

Market powerDefinitions

Pricing above marginal cost, without fear of entry

Market power
The power to raise price above marginal cost without fear that other firms will enter the market.
Monopoly
A firm with market power.

Note how low the bar is: “monopoly” here does not require being the only seller on earth. It requires only that nobody can readily undercut you.

  • GlaxoSmithKline’s patent gave it the exclusive right to make, use, and sell Combivir.
  • That exclusivity blocked competition — and the blocked competition is the market power.
  • Remove the patent, as India did, and the price collapses to marginal cost. Same drug, same factory costs, completely different price.
PART TWO

Why marginal revenue falls below price

The competitive firm could sell all it wanted at the market price. A firm with market power cannot — and the arithmetic of that is the heart of the chapter.

Marginal revenueThe logic

The price cut applies to every unit, not just the last one

MR < P for any firm facing a downward-sloping demand curve

THE COMPETITIVE CASE

MR = P

The firm is small enough to sell as much as it likes at the going price. One more unit adds exactly the price to revenue. Nothing is given up.

WITH MARKET POWER

MR < P

To sell one more unit the firm must cut the price — and the lower price applies to every unit, including the ones it could have sold at the old, higher price. The extra revenue is the new sale minus that loss on all the rest.

PART THREE

How a monopolist maximizes profit

The rule is the one from Chapter 11 — produce where MR = MC. What changes is where the price comes from.

Profit maximizationThe rule

Quantity off MR = MC. Price off the demand curve.

  • The profit-maximizing rule is unchanged: keep producing while the next unit adds more revenue than cost, and stop where MR = MC.
  • What changes is the price. The competitive firm read its price off a flat line. The monopolist reads it off the demand curve, at the quantity it chose.
  • Because MR lies below demand, that price sits above marginal cost — and the gap is the profit per unit.
The monopoly rule
Pick Q where MR = MC, then charge the price the demand curve will bear at that Q.π = (P − AC) × Q
Combivir, by the numbers
MR = MC at 80 million pills; price off demand $12.50; profit $10.00 a pill → $800 million.

Profit maximizationElasticity

The less price-sensitive the buyer, the bigger the markup

  • The more inelastic demand is, the larger the markup of price over marginal cost the firm can sustain.
  • Two effects make prescription-drug demand unusually inelastic:
    • “You can’t take it with you.” The seriously ill are not sensitive to the price of a life-saving drug.
    • “Other people’s money.” When an insurer pays the bill, the buyer barely registers the price.
The markup rule
At the profit-maximizing quantity, the proportional markup is the reciprocal of the elasticity of demand.(P − MC) / P = 1 / |e|

Combivir’s $12.50 price on a $2.50 average cost implies an elasticity of just 1.25 — demand barely more elastic than the point where a monopolist stops raising price at all.

The costs of monopolyCorruption & inefficiency

Many monopolies exist to move wealth, not to make anything

INDONESIA

The clove monopoly

The president’s son, Tommy Suharto, was handed the national monopoly on cloves — and spent the proceeds on Lamborghinis. No new product, no lower cost; just a transfer to someone politically connected.

ALGERIA

“General Wheat,” “General Tire”

Army generals each controlled a key input. Monopolies on inputs to other goods are especially damaging: every general grabs a bigger slice while the whole pie shrinks.

This is the part of monopoly that has no offsetting benefit at all — no innovation bought, no scale economy captured.

Natural monopoly
One firm can supply the whole market at lower cost than two or more could — water, natural gas, cable TV.
SourcesBarriers, networks, innovation

Three more ways to keep rivals out

Barriers to entry
Factors that raise the cost for new firms to enter an industry — hard-to-duplicate inputs, brands and trademarks, or an entrenched relationship with the market, like the TI-84 calculator.
Network effects
The product gets more valuable as more people use it. Facebook (everyone wants to be where their friends are); credit cards (buyers want acceptance, merchants want users).
Innovation
Products rivals cannot immediately copy.

The trade-off in one sentence: iPhones cost more than they would under stronger competition — but Apple would not innovate as hard without the prospect of monopoly profits.

Hold onto that sentence. It is the same bargain the patent system makes, and the one the next section has to price.

The benefitR&D incentives

What the deadweight loss buys

  • Developing a new drug costs over $1 billion. Patents — temporary monopolies — are one way to reward that spending.
  • Drug prices are lower in India (weak patents) and Canada (price controls). But if nobody grants patent profits, fewer new drugs get developed at all.
Prizes
Reward the R&D directly, without creating a monopoly at all.
Patent buyouts
Government buys the patent and releases it, cutting the price without cutting the R&D reward — though it must raise taxes to pay, and pricing a patent correctly is hard.
PolicyRegulating monopoly

Three levers, each with a catch

LEVER 1

Price controls

Unlike in a competitive market, a well-placed price ceiling on a monopoly can increase output and consumer surplus. The catch: it dulls the incentive to innovate.

LEVER 2

Government ownership

About 3,000 U.S. electric utilities — two-thirds government-owned, the rest heavily regulated — delivered cheap power for decades. The catch: without competition or a profit motive, inefficiency creeps in; and without regulation, providers exploit their power (California, 2000).

LEVER 3

Antitrust law

The Sherman Act (1890) and Clayton Act (1914) let government break up monopolies, block mergers, and stop anticompetitive acts. The catch: bigger firms bring scale efficiencies and more market power — regulators must weigh lower costs against higher prices.

Takeaway

What to carry out of Chapter 13

MR < P

A monopolist faces downward-sloping demand, so selling more means cutting price on every unit. It produces where MR = MC and prices off the demand curve.

The cost

Monopolies charge more, produce less, and shrink total surplus — a deadweight loss. The more inelastic the demand, the bigger the markup.

The bargain

Patents trade deadweight loss for innovation; natural monopolies trade it for economies of scale. Neither trade is free.

The policy

Price controls, public ownership and antitrust can all help — but the practice is far messier than the theory.

The warning

Many monopolies exist for no economic reason at all — simply to transfer wealth to politically powerful elites.
