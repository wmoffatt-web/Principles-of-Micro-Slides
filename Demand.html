As the price falls from $55 to $5, the quantity of oil demanded rises from 5 to 50 million barrels a day. This chapter covers why buyers respond to price, what they gain when they buy, and what else can change demand.

PART ONE

What is demand?

Demand describes how much buyers want at every possible price.

DemandDefinitions

Demand vs. quantity demanded

Demand curve
A function that shows the quantity demanded at different prices.
Quantity demanded
The quantity that buyers are willing and able to buy at a particular price.
Don’t mix these up
  • Quantity demanded is a single number at a single price. Ole Miss football: “How many tickets sell at $400?”
  • Demand is the relationship across all prices and quantities — the whole curve.
  • A change in the good’s own price moves you along the curve. A shifter moves the whole curve.
PART TWO

Marginal benefit and diminishing value

Why the demand curve slopes down.

Marginal benefitDiminishing value

Each extra unit is worth less than the last

Marginal benefit (marginal value)
The additional benefit a person gets from one more unit of a good.
Donut1st2nd3rd4th5th
How it feelsGreatGoodOKMehGross
  • Marginal benefit falls as you consume more.
  • Because each additional unit delivers less benefit, people will only buy more when the price drops.
  • That is the reason the demand curve slopes down.

The law of demandWhy it slopes down

Oil has uses of very different value

Law of demand
The demand curve is negatively sloped: when the price rises, quantity demanded falls; when the price falls, quantity demanded rises.
BUYER 1

The President

Air Force One flies at any price. This buyer barely responds to the price of oil at all.

BUYER 2

Delta Airlines

There is no substitute for jet fuel, so the airline keeps buying even when the price climbs.

BUYER 3

Frank, a retiree

He can take the bus or stay home. Very price-sensitive: he is the first to cut back.

When the price is high, only the highest-value uses buy oil. As it falls, lower-value uses — heating, plastic and rubber — switch back to oil, and quantity demanded rises.

PART FOUR

Consumer surplus

The difference between what buyers are willing to pay and what they actually pay.

Consumer surplusThe buyer’s gain

Willingness to pay minus price

Consumer surplus (CS)
The consumer’s gain from exchange — the difference between the maximum a buyer is willing to pay and the market price. Total CS is the area beneath the demand curve and above the price.

CS = ½ × height × base

Worked example — max willingness to pay $30
  • Height = max willingness to pay − market price. Base = quantity demanded.
  • If P = $10: CS = ½ × (30 − 10) × 70 = $700.
  • A lower price gives buyers a bigger surplus: a taller and wider triangle.

PART FIVE

Shifting demand vs. moving along it

A change in the good’s own price moves you along the curve. Other changes shift the curve.

ShiftsTwo different changes

Change in quantity demanded vs. change in demand

MOVEMENT ALONG THE CURVE

Change in quantity demanded

Caused only by a change in the good’s own price. You slide to a different point on the same curve.

SHIFT OF THE WHOLE CURVE

Change in demand

Caused by a shifter — income, population and so on — while the good’s own price is held constant. An increase shifts the curve right; a decrease shifts it left.

Ceteris paribus
“All else equal.” A demand curve is drawn holding everything except the good’s own price constant. A shifter is what happens when one of those other things changes.

After an increase in demand, people buy more at every price and will pay more for every quantity.

PART SIX

What shifts demand?

Six shifters: income, population, price of substitutes, price of complements, expectations and tastes.

Shifter 1Income

Normal goods and inferior goods

TypeIncome risesIncome falls
Normal goodDemand rises (right)Demand falls (left)
Inferior goodDemand falls (left)Demand rises (right)
  • Most goods are normal.
  • Inferior examples: Great Value instead of the name brand; domestic instead of craft beer.
Are children a normal good?
Black et al. (2013): when oil prices rise, coal prices rise too, so coal miners’ incomes rise. That lets researchers test how family size responds to income. Caveat: how income is distributed matters.
Shifters 2 & 3Population, price of substitutes

Population and the price of substitutes

POPULATION

A home-game weekend

More people generally means more demand, and a shift in sub-populations changes demand for specific goods. A home game against LSU floods town with people: at $20, the quantity of local coffee demanded jumps from 500 to 2,000.

Substitute
A good that can be consumed instead of another. A fall in a substitute’s price decreases demand for the good; a rise increases it.
  • Coke and Pepsi; Funky’s and the Library; any two sources of caffeine.
  • Price of orange juice rises → demand for apple juice increases.
  • Price of natural gas falls → demand for oil decreases.
Shifters 4 & 5Price of complements, expectations

Price of complements and expectations

Complement
Goods that go well together. A drop in a complement’s price increases demand for the good.
  • Price of iPhones rises → demand for iPhone apps falls.
  • Price of sugar falls → demand for tea rises.
Expectations
Expecting a future supply reduction — and so higher future prices — increases demand today.
  • Stocks and the efficient-market hypothesis: prices already reflect expectations.
  • Weather: an expected cold snap raises demand for heaters now. Expectations also drive seasonality.
Shifter 6Tastes

Tastes

  • Fads, fashions and advertising all shift demand.
  • Classic natural experiment: Catholic abstinence from meat during Lent raises demand for fish and lowers demand for beef.
Vatican II
When the year-round Friday fish rule was relaxed (1966), the demand for fish fell — documented in Ault et al. (1987). A change in tastes, with no change in the price of fish.

QUESTION 1

Max willingness to pay $30, P = $10, Q = 70. Consumer surplus?

½ × (30 − 10) × 70 = $700.

QUESTION 2

iPads are a normal good. Incomes rise. What happens?

Demand for iPads shifts right.

QUESTION 3

The price of orange juice rises. Demand for apple juice?

They are substitutes, so demand for apple juice increases.

QUESTION 4

The price of oil falls. Change in demand, or in quantity demanded?

Its own price changed: a movement along the curve, a change in quantity demanded.

Takeaway

What to carry out of Chapter 3

The law

A demand curve shows how buyers respond to price: higher price, less bought; lower price, more bought.

The reason

It slopes down because each extra unit is worth less: diminishing marginal benefit.

The gain

Consumer surplus is the buyer’s gain from exchange: the area under demand and above the price.

The shifters

The good’s own price moves you along the curve; income, population, related prices, expectations and tastes move the whole curve.
