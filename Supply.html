PART ONE

The supply curve and the law of supply

How much sellers offer at each price, and why they offer more when the price is higher.

SupplyDefinitions

Supply vs. quantity supplied

Supply curve
A function that shows the quantity supplied at different prices.
Quantity supplied
The quantity that sellers are willing and able to sell at a particular price.
Law of supply
The supply curve is positively sloped: as the price rises, quantity supplied rises, and as the price falls, quantity supplied falls.
Two ways to read a supply curve
  • Horizontally: at a given price, how much will sellers offer?
  • Vertically: to produce a given quantity, what price must sellers be paid?
SupplyWhy it slopes up

Producers have different costs

  • At the lowest price, only the lowest-cost producer can make a profit, so very little is supplied.
  • As the price rises, higher-cost producers can cover their costs, so more is produced.
  • At the highest prices, many producers find it worthwhile, and a large quantity is supplied.
  • For oil: as the price rises, it becomes profitable to extract oil from more costly sources.
How a firm picks its quantity
Quantity supplied is the output that maximizes profit at price P. A firm keeps producing until MC = MR, where the last unit just breaks even. In a competitive market MR = P, so the supply curve is the marginal-cost curve.Supply = MC

PART TWO

Producer surplus

The difference between the price sellers receive and the lowest price they would accept.

Producer surplusThe seller’s gain

Price minus the minimum a seller would accept

Producer surplus (PS)
The producer’s gain from exchange: the difference between the market price and the minimum price a seller would accept (its marginal cost). Total PS is the area above the supply curve and below the price.

PS = ½ × base × height

Worked example — minimum price $2
  • Base = quantity sold. Height = market price − minimum price.
  • At P* = $20 and Q* = 50: PS = ½ × (20 − 2) × 50 = $450.
  • Every unit before the last sold for more than it cost to make. Adding up those gaps gives producer surplus.

PART THREE

What shifts supply?

Technology and input prices, taxes and subsidies, expectations, entry and exit, and changes in opportunity cost.

ShiftsTwo different changes

Change in quantity supplied vs. change in supply

MOVEMENT ALONG THE CURVE

Change in quantity supplied

Caused by a change in the good’s own price.

SHIFT OF THE WHOLE CURVE

Change in supply

Caused by a shifter, such as costs or taxes, holding the good’s own price constant.

  • An increase in supply shifts the curve right. At each price sellers offer more, and at each quantity they will accept a lower price.
  • A decrease in supply shifts the curve left.
Shifters 1 & 2Technology and input prices, taxes and subsidies

Anything that changes costs shifts supply

Technology and input prices
Better technology lowers costs and increases supply. Higher input prices raise costs and decrease supply.
  • Faster car assembly raises the supply of cars (shift right).
  • Bourbon and corn: a drought cuts corn output and raises corn prices. Corn is an input to bourbon, so bourbon supply shifts left.
Taxes and subsidies
A per-unit tax works like a cost increase. It shifts supply up by the amount of the tax. A subsidy does the reverse and shifts supply down and to the right.
  • A $1-per-barrel tax on oil shifts S up to S + tax.
Shifters 3–5Expectations, entry and exit, opportunity cost

Expectations, entry and exit, opportunity cost

Expectations
If sellers expect higher prices later, they store goods and sell less today (shift left). If they expect lower prices later, they sell more now (shift right).
Entry or exit of producers
Entry increases supply: a new housing development in Oxford raises the supply of housing. Exit decreases supply: New Orleans housing after Hurricane Katrina.
Changes in opportunity cost
Producing one good means giving up another. If the price of wheat rises, growing soybeans costs more in lost wheat, so farmers switch to wheat and the supply of soybeans shifts left.

The same idea applies to college: if the wage premium from a degree falls, the payoff to enrolling instead of working falls. In a labor market, you, the worker, are on the supply side.

PART FOUR

Equilibrium

The price where the quantity buyers want equals the quantity sellers offer.

EquilibriumDefinitions

Where supply meets demand

Equilibrium
The price at which quantity demanded equals quantity supplied: the intersection of the supply and demand curves.
  • Equilibrium price and quantity are the only stable point in a free market.
  • At any other price, market forces push price and quantity back toward equilibrium.
Surplus (excess supply)
Quantity supplied > quantity demanded. Happens when the price is above equilibrium. Unsold goods pile up, so sellers cut the price.
Shortage (excess demand)
Quantity demanded > quantity supplied. Happens when the price is below equilibrium. Buyers compete for the scarce goods and bid the price up.

EquilibriumGains from trade

Equilibrium maximizes the gains from trade

At (P*, Q*), three things are true
  • The goods go to the buyers with the highest willingness to pay.
  • The goods are made by the sellers with the lowest costs.
  • There are no unexploited gains from trade and no wasteful trades.
Trading less than Q*
Some trades that would benefit both sides never happen. Those gains from trade are lost.
Trading more than Q*
The extra units cost more to make than buyers value them, so resources are wasted.

Evidence from the laboratory. In 1956 Vernon Smith tested the supply-and-demand model in lab experiments, and it predicted market behavior accurately. He won the 2002 Nobel Prize for making lab experiments a tool in economics.

Comparative statics

When supply or demand shifts, the equilibrium price and quantity change.

Watch the language
A change in demand or supply shifts the whole curve. A change in quantity demanded or quantity supplied is a movement along a fixed curve.

Application: shifts in world demand and supply explain the swings in the price of oil over time.

Questions to answer without notes

QUESTION 1

New technology speeds up car assembly. What happens to supply?

Supply of cars shifts right.

QUESTION 2

There is a surplus in a competitive market. What happens to price?

Price decreases. A shortage makes it increase.

QUESTION 3

Supply decreases. What happens to P* and Q*?

Price rises, quantity falls.

QUESTION 4

Demand decreases. What happens to P* and Q*?

Price falls, quantity falls.

Takeaway

Summary

Supply

Supply slopes up (the law of supply). Producer surplus, the area above supply and below the price, is the seller’s gain.

Equilibrium

A market settles where quantity demanded = quantity supplied, the only stable price.

Adjustment

A surplus pushes the price down. A shortage pushes the price up.

Gains

Total gains from trade (CS + PS) are largest at equilibrium.

Shifts

Demand up raises P and Q. Supply up lowers P and raises Q. A shift moves the whole curve; a movement is along it.
