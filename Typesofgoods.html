When markets work, and when they fail

Markets provide food, clothing and phones very well. But they underprovide some goods, like asteroid deflection and national defense, and overuse others, like ocean tuna.

PART ONE

Two questions that define a good

Can non-payers be kept out? Does one person’s use reduce another’s?

Defining goodsTwo properties

Excludable and rival

Excludable
Non-payers can be kept from using the good. Nonexcludable means they can’t easily be prevented from using it.
Rival
One person’s use reduces someone else’s ability to use it. Nonrival means many people can use the same unit at once.

Example of nonrival: asteroid deflection protects 7 billion people for the same cost as protecting 1 million.

Defining goodsA spectrum, not four boxes

Excludability and rivalry are matters of degree

  • The four boxes are a simplification. Real goods sit somewhere along two arrows.
  • Excludability depends on how costly it is to keep non-payers out: a password, a fence, a toll booth.
  • Rivalry depends on how crowded the good is: Wi-Fi is nonrival for a few users and rival when everyone streams at once.
  • A good can move along either arrow as technology or circumstances change.

PART TWO

Private goods vs. public goods

Why markets provide one well and underprovide the other.

Public goodsPrivate vs. public

Excluding non-payers is what gets goods produced

PRIVATE GOODS: EXCLUDABLE + RIVAL

Markets provide them well

Sellers can exclude non-payers, so there is an incentive to produce. The only people excluded are those unwilling or unable to pay the cost, and that isn’t inefficient.

PUBLIC GOODS: NONEXCLUDABLE + NONRIVAL

Markets underprovide them

You can’t easily charge users, so it is hard to get anyone to pay voluntarily.

Public goodsFree riders and forced riders

Free riders and forced riders

Free rider
Someone who enjoys a public good without paying a share of the cost.
Forced rider
Someone who pays for a public good (through taxes) but doesn’t value it.
  • Because public goods are nonexcludable, some people free ride, so the good is underprovided.
  • Taxing everyone to fund the good can make people better off, though it turns some into forced riders. In principle, government should provide the amount that maximizes total surplus.
  • Which property matters more? Nonexcludability is usually the bigger problem. It is what breaks the incentive to pay.

PART THREE

Club goods

Excludable but nonrival.

Club goodsExcludable + nonrival

Provided by markets, but inefficiently

Club good
A good that is excludable but nonrival, such as cable TV, streaming music or software. Also called a nonrival private good.
  • Markets can provide club goods, but some people who would pay the (near-zero) marginal cost, but not the market price, get excluded, even though letting them in costs almost nothing.
The peculiar case of advertising
Entrepreneurs find ways to profit from nonrival goods without charging users. Radio, TV and Google searches are free to users and paid for by advertisers. Wi-Fi may be sold, ad-funded, given away to attract customers, or supplied by local government.

PART FOUR

Common resources and the tragedy of the commons

Nonexcludable but rival.

Common resourcesNonexcludable + rival

Unowned resources get overused

Common resource
A good that is nonexcludable but rival: ocean tuna, the environment, public roads.
Tragedy of the commons
The tendency of any unowned (and so nonexcludable) resource to be overused and undermaintained.
  • Until they are caught, tuna are unowned. No one can be excluded, yet they are rival: your catch means less for me. That is not a public good.
  • Without ownership there is little incentive to conserve or maintain, because the benefits of restraint go mostly to others.
  • Since 1960, the tuna catch has fallen by about 75%.

Common resourcesSolutions

Solutions to the tragedy of the commons

COMMUNITY NORMS

Elinor Ostrom (Nobel 2009)

Groups around the world have avoided overfishing and overgrazing by enforcing shared norms. This gets harder as more unrelated users gain access.

COMMAND AND CONTROL

Can backfire

In 1968 British Columbia capped the number of fishing boats. Fishermen responded with “capital stuffing” (bigger engines, better electronics). Boat values tripled while the salmon fishery kept declining.

PROPERTY RIGHTS (ITQs)

New Zealand, 1986

Individual transferable quotas: each is a right to a set tonnage, the quotas sum to the total allowable catch, and they can be traded. It worked, and the fish catch rose.

Questions to answer without notes

QUESTION 1

What type of good is a fireworks display?

A public good: nonrival (your watching doesn’t dim mine) and nonexcludable (it’s hard to stop non-payers from looking up).

QUESTION 2

What do you call someone who enjoys a public good without paying?

A free rider.

QUESTION 3

For public goods, which property is usually the bigger problem?

Nonexcludability. It breaks the incentive to pay.

QUESTION 4

Why are club goods provided inefficiently?

Some people who would pay the near-zero marginal cost, but not the price, are excluded.

QUESTION 5

What is the tendency for common resources to be overused and undermaintained?

The tragedy of the commons.

QUESTION 6

What fixed New Zealand’s fisheries?

ITQs: individual transferable quotas, tradable rights to a set tonnage of catch.

Takeaway

Summary

Four types

Two properties, excludable and rival, sort goods into private goods, club goods, common resources and public goods.

Public goods

Valuable but undersupplied by markets. Nonexcludability is the bigger problem, and it is the main argument for government taxation and supply.

The commons

Common resources tend to be overused and undermaintained: the tragedy of the commons.

Fixes

Enforced norms or new property rights (ITQs) can prevent the tragedy where blunt limits fail.
